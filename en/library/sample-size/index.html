How many trades are enough to conclude anything?

Sample size is the number of trades used to judge a system; the smaller the sample, the more the measured average R swings, and the harder it is to tell a real edge from luck.

IntermediateAURORA StandardAURORA Pro7 min read
Topic: Research & ValidationMarket: All marketsAuthor: KenvinHoangReviewer: KapyBara✓ Reviewed 14 Oct 2026
The question

Thirty trades with an average of +0.20R: is that enough to trust the system has an edge?

After this lesson you will

  • Understand why the average R of a small sample swings widely
  • Estimate a confidence range for average R
  • Know when to wait rather than rush to a conclusion

What is sample size, and why does it matter?

Sample size is the number of trades used to judge a system. Every trade has a different result (+1.5R, 0R, −1R…), so the average R of a small group of trades swings widely through luck alone. The larger the sample, the closer the measured average gets to the true expectancy.

The error of average R

A simple estimate:

Standard error ≈ Standard deviation of R per trade ÷ √(number of trades)

The plausible range (about 95%) of the true average R is measured average R ± 2 standard errors.

Example: a measured average of +0.20R, with a per-trade standard deviation of about 1.2R (reasonable for a +1.5R / 0R / −1R plan):

Trades Standard error Plausible range of true average R Conclusion
30 0.22R −0.23R … +0.63R Cannot be told apart from 0
100 0.12R −0.04R … +0.44R Still touches 0
400 0.06R +0.08R … +0.32R Basis to believe it is positive

Common misunderstandings

  • 30 trades is enough. 20–30 trades is the minimum to start looking, not to conclude.
  • Win rate settles quickly. Win rate swings too; 50% wins over 30 trades could be a 40% or a 60% system.
  • Split the data to find a bright spot. Splitting by session, score or setup leaves each group with few trades; searching many small groups will surely find a “great” one by chance.
  • Good results on one symbol are enough. Pooling several similar symbols grows the sample and reduces the risk of luck.

Applying it when reading AURORA statistics

  • Always read the trade count first on the dashboard; rows with few trades are hints, not conclusions.

  • The sub-rows with the fewest trades:

    • Score ≥ 70, session rows, ⛔ Filtered.
    • In AURORA Pro: 💎 Sweep, RETEST / SWEEP / IFVG.

    These are where false differences appear most easily.

  • Strategy Validator across symbols: export the trade lists of several similar symbols to get a bigger sample when analysing Q and T3 score buckets.

  • Combine with out-of-sample testing: choose a configuration on in-sample data and confirm it out-of-sample.

Limits

  • The formula assumes independent trades and unchanging market conditions. In reality markets change regime, so even a large sample from one period may not represent the future.
  • 1.2R is an example. Calculate the real standard deviation from your own trade list.
  • A large sample cannot fix bad data. Costs, data sources and calculations must be right first.

Practical checklist

  • Write the trade count next to every statistic.
  • Treat 20–30 trades as the minimum to start looking, not to conclude.
  • Estimate a range of about ±2 standard errors around average R.
  • Pool data from several symbols of the same market type to grow the sample.
  • The more you split groups (by session, by score), the more trades each group needs.

Thirty trades, average +0.20R. It sounds like an edge. But each trade can win 1.5R or lose 1R, so the average of a small sample swings a lot. If each trade has a standard deviation of about 1.2R, then with 30 trades the plausible range for the true average is roughly minus 0.23R to plus 0.63R. That range still includes zero, so it cannot yet be told apart from no edge. With 400 trades, the range narrows to roughly plus 0.08R to plus 0.32R; only then is there a basis for trust. So 20 to 30 trades is only where you start looking. The more you split the data into groups, the more trades each group needs.
